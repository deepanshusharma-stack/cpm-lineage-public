CPM Lineage: A Deterministic, Auditable Decision Framework for CPM-Billed Advertising

Deepanshu Sharma

Describes the open-source Python library cpm_lineage, version [[version]]. All measurements in this paper are on synthetic data.

Abstract Many ad platforms bill per thousand impressions (CPM) and report few or no clicks, so optimisation routines written around cost per click and click-through rate do not apply to them, and the automation such platforms offer rarely states why it acts. We describe cpm_lineage, a library that diagnoses and plans for a CPM-billed account under one constraint: every recommendation must trace to a number computed from the advertiser's own exports and to a named threshold. The library has two layers. The first is exact: return on ad spend factors into conversions per impression, selling price and CPM, and the log of any change splits into three terms with zero residual; midpoint shift-share extends this to any grain. The second is estimated and labelled as such: a sign-constrained ridge model pooled toward account-level elasticities attributes the change to eleven levers and is accepted only when an independent first-difference model agrees; a two-parameter saturation curve gates budget raises at the point where marginal return falls to one. Rule families turn these readings into action cards; a fixed priority table resolves contradictions or abstains; an ordered gate stack gives every card one of five typed states; a ledger scores outcomes, including those of cards that were not taken. The library is pure Python with numpy as its only dependency. On [[n_seeds]] seeded synthetic datasets with five planted events, all five were found in every run with [[extra_a]] findings on unplanted subjects, and a world with the events removed produced none; residuals of the log identity and of the ROAS shift-shares stayed below 10−14; output was identical under repetition and row shuffling. A second, differently shaped platform ran through an adapter without library changes. We also report where the method failed or over-acted on synthetic data, and state plainly what synthetic tests cannot show: the thresholds are judgment defaults, and nothing here measures performance on a real account.

1 Introduction

Search advertising practice grew up around the click. Bids are set per click, the funnel is read as impressions, click-through rate and conversion rate, and most published optimisation routines assume that all three are observed. A large class of platforms does not fit this picture: quick-commerce marketplaces, retail media networks and display exchanges commonly charge per thousand impressions (CPM), and several report no click at all. On such a platform the advertiser pays for being shown, the price paid per impression moves with the auction rather than with the advertiser's own conversion, and a click-based routine has nothing to hold on to.

The second difficulty is opacity. Platform-side and third-party automation usually emits a recommendation or simply changes a bid, without the evidence behind it. An operator who is asked to approve a budget increase cannot check whether the increase follows from the data, from a default, or from a model's extrapolation. When two automated recommendations contradict each other on the same campaign, it is often not recorded which one won or why.

This paper describes a library built under one design constraint: every recommendation traces to a number and a named threshold. Concretely, (i) every quantity is computed by a pure function from plain records, with no network or database access; (ii) every threshold is a named field of a frozen configuration dataclass with a default and a one-line rationale ([[n_cfg_fields]] fields across [[n_cfg_cls]] configuration and platform-profile dataclasses at the time of writing, as listed in docs/thresholds.md, counting labels and vocabularies as well as numeric thresholds); (iii) every finding names the module whose numbers it carries; (iv) every action card carries its trigger sentence, its evidence mapping, a confidence score that is a sum of labelled parts, and a typed state; and (v) declining to act is a recorded outcome with a reason code, never silence. No learned policy takes part in the decision. The estimated components (a ridge regression and a curve fit) are fitted deterministically and their output is used only through thresholds.

The contributions are: an exact three-channel identity for return on ad spend under CPM billing and a family of exact decompositions around it (Section 3); a two-model attribution with an explicit agreement test and an abstaining verdict (Section 4); a budget gate based on marginal rather than average return (Section 5); CPM and bidder audits (Section 6); a deterministic action layer with a conflict table that can abstain (Section 7); an outcome ledger that also scores untaken actions (Section 8); a platform abstraction (Section 9); and a reproducible evaluation on synthetic data with planted events (Section 10), followed by the limits of all of the above (Section 11). Figure 1 shows the whole path.

2 Related work

Attribution and Shapley values. The Shapley value [1] assigns a cooperative game's payoff to its players and is the basis of model-explanation methods that attribute a prediction to features [2, 3]. In advertising, data-driven attribution assigns conversions to touchpoints [4, 5]. Our use is narrower: we attribute the change of a metric between two windows to the change of each lever. For a model that is linear in its inputs this value has the closed form coefficient times change, which the default path uses; exact enumeration is kept for non-linear models.

Shift-share and index decomposition. Splitting the change of an aggregate rate into a composition part and a within-group part goes back to shift-share analysis in regional science [6] and to wage decompositions [7], where the result depends on which period's weights are used. Index decomposition analysis treats the same problem for products of factors; logarithmic methods are preferred there because they leave no residual [8], and the log-change index numbers of Sato and Vartia [9, 10] give exact additive forms. Our identity layer is a log decomposition of a three-factor product; our shift-share uses midpoint weights, which removes the interaction term.

Media-mix models and saturation. Media-mix models regress sales on spend with carry-over (“adstock” [11]) and a concave response [12], and their difficulties with correlated inputs and limited variation are well documented [13]. We fit a two-parameter concave curve per campaign for one purpose only, to locate where marginal return falls to one, and treat it as correlational.

Auction theory. In a second-price auction a winner pays the next bid [14, 15]; the generalized second-price and position auctions used for sponsored listings extend this to ranked slots [16, 17]. Many display and marketplace auctions are first-price, where the winner pays its own bid. The library records the auction type per platform and applies one check (Section 6) only under second-price rules.

Rule-based and learned bidding. Learned bidding optimises a bid function or policy from logged auctions [18, 19, 20]; reviews of display markets describe the setting [21]. Such systems can outperform fixed rules where data are plentiful, but their decisions are hard to audit. Our position follows the argument that interpretable models are preferable where a human must sign off on the decision [22]. Field experiments show that advertising effects are hard to measure and that observational estimates can differ widely from experimental ones [23, 24, 25]; we therefore label attribution as observational and build a holdout and a difference-in-differences read [26, 27] into the feedback loop.

3 The CPM identity and exact decomposition

For any scope (account, campaign, keyword) and window, let S be spend, R attributed sales, I impressions and U units sold. Define three channels, each a ratio of window sums:

CPM=1000SI, ICR=1000UI, ASP=RU
(1)

CPM is cost per thousand impressions, ICR (impression conversion rate) is units per thousand impressions (icr1k in the code and guides), and ASP is the average selling price. Return on ad spend (ROAS) is R/S. Multiplying and dividing by U and I, the impression count and the unit count cancel:

ROAS=RS= (1000U/I)⋅(R/U) 1000S/I= ICR⋅ASPCPM
(2)

No click appears. Where clicks exist, ICR is the product of click-through and conversion rates (times the same 1000) and nothing else changes. Taking logarithms of (2) in a baseline window b and a comparison window c and subtracting, with Δ ln X = ln Xc − ln Xb:

ΔlnROAS= ΔlnICR+ ΔlnASP− ΔlnCPM
(3)

Equation (3) is an identity, not a model: it holds to floating-point precision whenever all four sums are positive in both windows. We call it layer 1. Each term is a signed contribution in log points, and a channel's share of the move is its term divided by the left-hand side. Three implementation rules keep it exact. Ratios are always computed from window sums, never as a mean of daily ratios. Every spend day is included, including days with no sale. And a channel value is floored at 10−9 before the logarithm; if the floor is hit the result is flagged as not exact rather than reported as if it were. When the net move is close to zero (|Δ ln ROAS| ≤ 10−4) the shares would divide by almost nothing, so a fallback denominator is used and callers can suppress shares altogether. Figure 2 shows the decomposition from one run.

3.1 A first read from the three channels

For a ROAS fall with enough spend, a decision table names the channel with the most negative term. If it is CPM, the bid change separates four cases at a 7% band: bid flat (“auction pressure”), bid raised (“self-inflicted”), bid cut (pressure despite a lower bid) and no bid data (stated as unknown). If it is ICR the read is “conversion fell”; otherwise “selling price fell”. This is a classification of an exact split, with no claim about cause.

3.2 Mix against rate at any grain

A blended rate R = Σvalue/Σweight (ROAS with spend as weight, ASP with units as weight, CPM with impressions as weight) can be written as Σi wiri over items i with weight share wi and own rate ri. For each item the product rule with midpoint weights gives

wicric− wibrib= (wic−wib) ric+rib2⏟mix + wic+wib2 (ric−rib)⏟rate
(4)

which can be checked by expanding the right-hand side: the cross terms cancel. Summing (4) over items gives Rc − Rb with no interaction term. One function implements (4); shift-share by ad type, campaign, keyword, product or geography is the same call with a different key, and the price split of an ASP move (product mix against own-price change) is the same call with units as the weight. An item present in only one window is handled by a stated convention (missing rate taken as zero, or the single available rate carried), both exact. Value recorded against an item with no weight cannot enter a share identity and is reported separately instead of being folded in.

Two further exact splits are provided. The prior-rate two-factor split of a product V·r, (Vc − Vb)rb + Vc(rc − rb), assigns the interaction to the rate effect and is used for units (impressions × conversion) and value (units × price); it is a different convention from (4) and the two are never mixed inside one identity. The entry/exit split separates the effect of continuing entities from that of entities that started or stopped, measuring an entrant against the prior blended ROAS so that a weak launch reads as a drag; its fourth term (re-weighting among continuing entities) is defined as the remainder. A Pareto ranking of spend × (reference ROAS − entity ROAS) orders where to look first; it is a ranking device, since against a reference computed on the same set the drags sum to zero.

4 Lever attribution

Layer 1 says through which channel ROAS moved. Layer 2 asks which observable conditions moved with it. A lever is one scalar per entity and day. There are eleven, in two tiers. Eight tier-1 levers are things an advertiser controls: bid, ad position, own paid share of voice, own organic share of voice, own availability, share of spend on competitor-targeted units, own discount, and the own-to-competitor price ratio. Three tier-2 levers are outside conditions: the log of total impressions on the entity's targeting units (a demand control), competitor availability and competitor discount. Tier-2 levers stay in the model to reduce omitted-variable bias but can never be the subject of an action; they only produce a context note. Two further rules shape the set: mirrored quantities are modelled once (competitor paid share is one minus own), and only n − 1 members of a partition enter.

Preparation. Out-of-range values (a position above 500, a share outside [0, 1]) become missing. A lever enters the model only if more than 20% of days carry a real value, and is removed if more than 40% would be imputed; a variance-inflation check may fold the two discounts into the price ratio. With fewer than four usable levers no entity model is fitted. Data volume routes the method: under 21 active days there is no move model at all, 21–34 days use a short mode with seven fast-moving levers, and 35 or more use the full set. The panel is clipped to the two compared windows, so a week-over-week comparison returns “thin data” instead of a fit on history outside the windows.

Backbone. For each channel k the daily log channel yk is regressed on z-scored levers Z by a ridge regression [28] shrunk toward account-level coefficients β0 (fitted once on the pooled panel) with box constraints on signs that economics fixes:

wk= arg minlo≤w≤hi ‖(Z−z¯)w− (yk−y¯k)‖2 +λ ‖w−β0,k‖2
(5)

The sign constraints are seven: on ICR, availability, paid share and discount cannot have a negative coefficient and position and price ratio cannot have a positive one; on CPM, bid cannot be negative; on ASP, discount cannot be positive. The position constraint flips for platforms where a higher number is better. The problem is strictly convex and is solved by cyclic coordinate descent, so the result does not depend on a random start. The strength λ is chosen from {1, 4, 16, 64} by blocked, expanding-window cross-validation in full mode and fixed at 16 in short mode. Because the model is linear in z, lever j's contribution to the log ROAS move is

cj=∑k sk wk,j (z¯jc− z¯jb), sICR=sASP=+1, sCPM=−1
(6)

which equals the Shapley value of the fitted model for the move from baseline to comparison means.

Cross-check. A levels regression can be confounded by slow trends. A second, independent ridge is therefore fitted on day-to-day first differences of the same series (optionally with a geometric carry-over on visibility levers, accepted only if it beats the raw model's skill by a margin). Its one-step-ahead skill against persistence is 1 − SSEmodel/SSEpersistence: zero means no better than “tomorrow equals today”. The skill reported is always that of the raw difference model, to avoid reporting the best of several variants scored on the same folds.

Jury and verdict. Each lever gets a jury label: confirm when both models assign it at least 5% of the move with the same sign, contest when both are material with opposite signs, solo when only the backbone is. One attribution verdict then covers the whole reading, first match winning: thin-data; unexplained when the model explains less than 20% of the move, when the move is small but lever activity is large and offsetting, when the largest lever is contested, or when skill is below −0.25; confirmed when the largest lever is confirmed and skill is at least zero; otherwise directional. Only confirmed and directional verdicts allow a lever story and elasticity-sized test steps. In the run of Figure 2 the bid lever received [[bid_contrib]] log points from the backbone and [[bid_delta]] from the difference model ([[bid_pct]]% of the move, label confirm, move skill [[move_skill]]; net explained [[expl_net]]%), giving confirmed.

Exact Shapley as the alternative. For a non-linear response the library offers exact Shapley values by enumerating all 2n coalitions of lever changes,

φj= ∑S⊆N∖{j} |S|! (n−|S|−1)!n! [f(S∪{j})−f(S)]
(7)

where f(S) is the model's prediction with the levers in S at their comparison means and the rest at baseline. It is limited to 18 levers and ships with a small numpy-only gradient-boosted tree model [29]. The values sum to the model's predicted change, not the observed one; the gap is reported as a residual.

5 Budget and saturation

A campaign is capped when it has at least 7 active days and spends at least 90% of its daily budget on at least 60% of them. Whether to raise a capped budget depends on what the next unit of spend returns, not on the average. The library fits, per entity, a two-parameter concave curve through the origin to about 90 daily points:

sales(x)=b ln⁡ (1+xθ), m(x)= d salesdx= bθ+x, m(x*)=1⇔ x*=b−θ
(8)

Here x is daily spend and m the marginal ROAS. For fixed θ the best b is a closed-form ratio, so fitting is a one-dimensional search over θ (a log grid refined by golden section) and is deterministic. A fit is refused, with a reason code, for fewer than 21 spending days, a spend coefficient of variation under 0.15, no positive spend-sales correlation, a level shift confounded with time, or an in-sample R² under 0.05. Only fits with R² of at least 0.3 may block a raise or set a ceiling. A capped campaign never varies its spend, so its own curve is usually refused; an account-pooled curve may then lend a marginal ROAS but never a ceiling.

The gate (Figure 3) is then: a raise is blocked when the marginal at the cap is below 0.9, held when it is below 1.0, and otherwise sized as a range of 1.3 to 2.0 times the cap whose upper end is clamped to x*. The bar is a marginal return of one sales unit per spend unit; with the default economics this equals the breakeven ROAS, but unlike the average-ROAS bars it is not rescaled when an account sets a different breakeven. An ordered table turns the probe into one verb. In order: ended campaign (no action), unknown ROAS (investigate tracking; never raise blind), operator schedule mistaken for exhaustion, availability below the block line, at least 40% of spend below breakeven (fix the bleed first), the marginal gate, organic share of at least 70%, and only then a raise; ROAS between breakeven and twice breakeven gets “hold and monitor”, below breakeven “tighten”.

Three money estimators. A currency figure is put on a budget action in three ways: a stake with expected value from average ROAS and a success probability; the difference of two points on the fitted curve; and a finite-difference marginal from the entity's high-spend against low-spend days. They share no model and can give three different numbers for the same raise. The library does not reconcile them. Each figure carries an estimator label, and a comparison function returns all three with reconciled: False.

6 CPM per position, the bid-agent audit and overpay hypotheses

CPM per observed position. Rows are bucketed by observed ad position and each bucket's CPM is computed from its own spend and impressions, for two windows. Only positions at which the ad was actually seen are shown; nothing is interpolated. A four-way classifier explains a keyword's CPM rise above 5% with first match winning: bought position (position improved by 0.3 slots or own ad share rose 5 points), rival pressure (a rival's ad share rose 3 points), worse spot (position worsened by 0.3 slots), else auction price.

Bid-agent audit. Some advertisers run a position-targeting bidder, a closed loop that reads the ad's position and moves the bid to hold a target range under a ceiling, ceiling = base bid × (1 + g/100) for a guardrail percentage g. From the bidder's decision log the audit computes, per rule set, the share of decisions at the ceiling (cap), the share that reached the target (hit) and the share in which the position was found at all (seen). A rule set is flagged blind when seen is under 60%, and hot when it is not blind, capped on at least 30% of decisions and hitting on fewer than half. Rates are decision-weighted, and a counter that the log does not carry stays unknown instead of being treated as zero.

Three overpay hypotheses. For one day and a keyword list, each campaign × keyword × geo row is tested independently. H1, the bidder is not optimising: blind; better than target on at least half the decisions without a bid cut; pinned at the ceiling; or a bid range under 2% of its median over at least ten decisions. H2, our own bid is our floor: under second-price rules the next bidder beneath sets the price, so if that bidder is the advertiser's own second campaign on the same keyword and geo, bidding at least 80% of the row's CPM, the advertiser sets its own price. H2 runs only when the platform profile declares a second-price auction and bids quoted per thousand impressions, and says so when skipped. H3, CPM is high for the position: CPM above 1.25 times the median of the advertiser's own rows in the same position band that day, with at least three rows.

7 The action layer

Rule families. Eight families are pure functions from an aggregated context to candidate action cards: efficiency (budget cuts and raises, keyword pauses), growth, defense, bid, experiment, share-of-voice gap, geo, and collision (two own campaigns bidding one keyword in the same auction). A card states family, lever type, direction, the change, the trigger sentence, the evidence numbers and a stake in currency. Families neither veto nor rank. Three rule profiles select which rules run: classic judges against breakeven multiples, marginal also re-sizes budget cards on the finite-difference marginal, and verdict judges each keyword and keyword-led campaign against the average ROAS of its role bucket, floored at breakeven.

Pipeline. The order is fixed. A holdout is drawn first: an entity is held when the first eight hex digits of SHA-1(entity id|period), modulo 100, fall below 15, which is stable within a month and rotates after it; held entities receive no cards and are listed on the plan as a standing comparison group (the list can be passed as the control of the difference-in-differences read in Section 8; the library does not make that comparison on its own). Dead entities are removed. Three vetoes follow: an entity under a running experiment, a raise on an entity that is not budget-bound or whose amounts cannot be stated, and a lever that the entity's ad type does not have. Then the conflict table, confidence and expected value, one operator-stated run check (a ROAS floor or a spend ceiling; any other check is recorded as not machine-enforced), an equity gate that stops cuts on a brand-carrying entity while share is being lost, and per-family caps applied last so that a vetoed card never uses a slot. A global cap of 25 pending cards keeps a plan reviewable in one sitting.

Conflict table and abstention. Duplicates are merged, keeping the card of the highest-priority family. For bid cards on one term, the card with more evidence spend wins. For budget-grain contradictions on one entity (a raise against a cut or a pausing experiment) the winner is the family with the highest fixed priority (Figure 4). Cards on the opposing side are marked superseded and kept on record, unless one of them carries a stake of more than three times the winner's. In that case the table abstains: every card in the group becomes a tension group in which a human may apply exactly one. Keyword and bid cards never enter this table; a budget cut, keyword pauses and a bid move on one entity are treated as one sequence and ordered into waves (cuts first, then bids and experiments, then raises) so that outcomes remain attributable.

Confidence. Confidence is a bounded sum of labelled parts, clamp(40 + Σ parts, 25, 90): evidence window length (+15 or +8), spend depth (up to +10), one clarity term by card type (for a budget card, up to +15 by distance of ROAS from breakeven), availability penalties, a penalty of 10 for an entity under 14 days old, and, once a family has at least five paired outcomes, up to ±10 from its realised win rate and up to ±8 from precedent on the same entity. The parts are stored on the card. Expected value per day is an estimated daily effect times a success probability, 0.5 until five outcomes exist; figures that are market-opportunity estimates are tagged so that they never rank ahead of money actually moved.

Typed verdicts. Finally every card passes an ordered gate stack, the verdict spine, and leaves with a state and reason code. The stages, in order, with the first claim winning: integrity (contaminated cohorts and placeholder budgets), lifecycle (no cut within 5 days of launch), evidence (under 1,000 window impressions), identity (a discount swing of 8 points holds media verdicts), action lock (one open action per entity for 7 days), automation (a lever owned by an automation is handed to a human), risk bars, terminals. The states are ACT (ship), OBSERVE (the read is not trustworthy yet), HOLD (a time-bound condition must pass), ESCALATE (a human must decide), and NO-ACTION (decided elsewhere, such as a conflict loser). Risk bars scale the confidence required with the cost of being wrong: 25 for a watch, 45 for a bid change, 55 for a budget raise, 65 for any cut, plus 10 while the whole book is under 21 days old. Before the gates, cards are reshaped toward the cheapest reversible lever (a keyword pause becomes a 25% bid cut with a re-read after 5 days; a raise is trimmed to 25% steps), and sweeps add cards for things the families cannot see, such as a profitable campaign that stopped serving or spend on an ad type with no attributed sales. An ACT that cannot fill its required fields degrades to OBSERVE.

8 The feedback loop

Ledger. When a plan is saved, the ledger writes one row per card and horizon (7, 14 and 30 days) for every card, including vetoed cards and conflict losers. Operators mark cards applied or skipped; a card that lost a conflict cannot be marked applied, and re-saving a regenerated plan keeps operator marks. Storage is behind a small backend interface with in-memory and SQLite implementations.

Outcome pairing. Once the feed covers a horizon h after the action date, ROAS over the h days after is compared with ROAS over the h days before. The expectation is the before-value adjusted for the market tide, the ratio of the segment's total sales over the same two windows, damped by a square root because ad efficiency does not follow market volume one for one:

expected=ROASbefore⋅ clamp(tide,0.5,2)
(9)

A cut is On Track when efficiency holds at 95% of expectation and at least 60% of revenue is kept, otherwise Review; a raise is On Track at 85% of expectation, otherwise Off Track. The asymmetry is deliberate: a cut that misses still saved the money. Other labels are Too early, No signal, and Unreadable when a second action on the same entity landed inside the window. Cards that were not taken are scored as counterfactuals (did efficiency go on to fall, as the cut card said it would?), which lets the priority table itself be audited. Only On Track, Review and Off Track at the 7-day horizon feed the family win rates used in Section 7.

Difference-in-differences. For a logged recommendation on an entity, with a window W of at most 14 days on each side of the issue day:

DiD= (ROASposttreated−ROASpretreated) − (ROASpostcontrol−ROASprecontrol)
(10)

where the control is every other entity in the account (or a supplied list), always excluding the treated one. The read is landed above +0.10 ROAS points, worsened below −0.10, flat between, and no-data when it cannot be measured; an unmeasurable read is never called flat. A taken-detection step checks the platform change log (or, failing that, spend-share shifts) for evidence that the recommended change was actually made. This is a descriptive contrast with a non-randomised control, not an experiment.

9 Platform abstraction

Nothing platform-specific is hard-coded. A platform is described once by a PlatformProfile and its data are converted by an adapter into neutral records. The contract is small:

  • Required: delivery rows with date, entity id, spend and impressions, and normally sales and units. Rows may be at any finer grain (targeting unit, geo, product, hour); repeated keys are summed.
  • Optional feeds, each unlocking named functions: budget pacing (cap tests, curve, raise sizing), share of voice (rival pressure, organic share), shelf (availability and price levers), market sell-out (share and root-cause reads), change log (taken-detection), bidder decision log (Section 6). A missing feed produces a “not assessed” note, never an error or a silent pass.
  • Declared semantics: ad types by capability (keyword auction, auto-targeted retargeting with an attribution deflator, reach placement with no attributed sales) and the levers each allows; the raw-to-canonical status map; whether a position field exists and which direction is better; auction type and bid unit; geo grain and long-tail roll-up labels that are never a target; which targeting types are biddable; trading-day hours; and a currency scale.

Money thresholds are written in reference units and multiplied by the currency scale; profitability bars are multiples of the effective breakeven. Rules that need a capability the platform lacks abstain: without a position field the per-keyword bid rules and position levers are silent, and the self-floor check does not run outside second-price auctions. The words “keyword” and “campaign” in output mean “targeting unit” and “entity”; this vocabulary is fixed in the current API.

10 Evaluation on synthetic data with planted events

No results from real accounts exist for this library, and none are claimed. The evaluation answers a narrower question: on data whose ground truth is known by construction, does the library find what was planted, stay silent when nothing was, keep its exactness and determinism claims, and run on a differently shaped platform? Everything below is produced by one script, docs/paper/experiments.py, with seeds 1 to [[n_seeds]] (1 to [[n_dose]] for the dose-response sweep, 1 to [[n_det]] for determinism); the figures use seed 7. To reproduce (Python [[python]], numpy [[numpy]]; about four minutes on one core):

pip install -e . && python docs/paper/experiments.py

Results are written to docs/paper/results.json, from which the tables and figures in this paper are generated by docs/paper/build_paper.py.

Worlds. The marketplace world is the library's generator: 90 days, one advertiser, nine campaigns on a first-price CPM platform with no clicks, four city geos plus a long-tail bucket, and all feeds ([[rows_a]] delivery rows per dataset). Impressions and CPM carry lognormal noise, units are Poisson. Five events are planted (Table 1). The generator and the detectors were written together, so this is a test of plumbing and of the thresholds' behaviour near their edges, not an independent benchmark. The second platform is defined entirely in examples/custom_platform_adapter.py: 70 days, five line items, a second-price auction, clicks, no position field, country geos, orders instead of units, money in micros with amounts about 100 times smaller, served minutes instead of an exhaustion time, and only the delivery, entity, targeting and pacing feeds ([[rows_f]] rows). Two events are planted: a 50% CPM rise on one query and a profitable line item that serves a third of the day.

Dose-response. Detection at full size says little about where a detector stops working, so each event was re-planted at several sizes (Figure 5). The transitions sit where the named thresholds put them. A CPM rise is reported above 5%: at a multiplier of 1.05 it was found in [[cpm5]] runs and from 1.08 always. A keyword is called a bleeder below ROAS 1: at a true ROAS of exactly 1.0, noise put it under the bar in [[ble1]] runs. The availability detector is the least sensitive: a fall to 0.75 on one of four products in one geo, for 21 of the 30 days, was missed in every run, because the test is on the geo-level mean across products and requires a fall of 0.05. A single-product stock-out can therefore pass unreported at the default setting.

11 Limitations and threats to validity

  • Thresholds are judgment defaults. The numeric thresholds among the [[n_cfg_fields]] configuration fields are round numbers chosen by judgment. None is calibrated or back-tested here. The dose-response results show that outcomes near a threshold depend on it directly; the defaults should be treated as starting points and overridden per account.
  • Synthetic evaluation only. The generator encodes the same view of the world as the rules, and was written alongside them. Finding planted events shows that the plumbing is sound and that the thresholds behave as stated. It shows nothing about real accounts, where events overlap, feeds are late or restated, and noise is not lognormal. We make no claim about uplift, accuracy or scale in practice.
  • Attribution is observational. The verdict “confirmed” means that two regressions on the same observational data agree on the largest lever and that the difference model is no worse than persistence. It is not a causal estimate. Levers move together, the advertiser's own actions respond to outcomes, and Section 10 shows lever stories on pure noise in [[ws_story]] of [[n_seeds]] runs. Imputation medians use both windows.
  • The three money estimators are not reconciled. The same raise can carry three figures. They are labelled, never averaged, and no evidence is offered that any of them predicts realised revenue.
  • The saturation curve is correlational. It has no demand controls, uses same-day attribution with no carry-over, reports in-sample fit without intervals, and extrapolates by functional form beyond observed spend. A capped campaign cannot identify its own curve. The marginal bar is a literal one and ignores a configured breakeven.
  • The self-floor check assumes second-price pricing. H2 assumes that the next bid beneath sets the price paid. This has not been verified against billed prices on any platform, and reserve prices, quality adjustments or hybrid auction rules would break it. H3 uses the advertiser's own rows as the reference, so it detects inconsistency between the advertiser's geos and campaigns, not overpayment against the market. “Clearing bids” from a bidder log are bids set, not prices paid.
  • Fixed order decides. Several components are first-match cascades (the raise verb, the CPM classifier, the gate stack, the root-cause list). The first condition to fire names the cause; co-occurring causes are not weighed against each other, and two readers may describe one event differently (Table 5).
  • Relative bars need a population. The verdict profile's bucket-average bar over-acts on small accounts (Table 6).
  • The feedback loop is untested end to end. Outcome labels, win rates and the difference-in-differences read are unit-tested but were not exercised by the single-shot experiments here, because that needs operator decisions and later data. By default the control group of that read is the rest of the account, which is not randomised; the hash-drawn holdout is drawn and reported but no holdout-against-treated comparison is computed for the caller. The tide adjustment is a heuristic.
  • Scope. One advertiser at a time, daily grain for most functions, and platform data are trusted as delivered; restatement detection exists but does not correct anything. Attributed sales are taken at the platform's definition, corrected only by a fixed deflator on auto-targeted types.

12 Conclusion

Under CPM billing, ROAS factors exactly into conversions per impression, price and cost per impression, and that identity is enough to anchor a diagnosis that needs no click data. Around it we built a library in which the exact parts are kept exact, the estimated parts are labelled and gated by an agreement test, contradictions are resolved by a fixed table that can abstain, and every action leaves with a typed state and the numbers behind it. On synthetic data the library found what was planted, stayed silent when nothing was, and reproduced its output exactly; the same experiments exposed a detector that misses moderate single-product stock-outs, lever stories told on noise, and a rule profile that over-acts on small accounts. The method's value on real accounts is an open question that only measured outcomes can answer, and the ledger and holdout are there so that they can be measured.
